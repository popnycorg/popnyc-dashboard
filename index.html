The reason on your denial letter or EOB tells you your next move. Find yours below. Then head to Appeal Packets or Templates.
What the denial may say What it usually means What to do first
Prior authorization required / no authorization The plan says approval was needed before the service, scan, surgery, or medication. Ask the provider whether they requested auth, get the denial notice, and ask for a retro authorization or medical-necessity review if appropriate.
Not medically necessary The insurer says the records did not prove the service was needed under plan rules. Ask your doctor for chart notes, test results, treatment history, and a letter explaining why the service was necessary.
Out-of-network The claim was processed using non-contracted benefits, or the plan says the provider or facility was outside the network. Verify network status for the facility and every clinician involved, especially anesthesia, labs, pathology, and imaging.
Service not covered / plan exclusion The plan says this benefit is excluded from the contract. Check the Summary Plan Description, Evidence of Coverage, or benefit booklet to confirm whether the exclusion is real or misapplied.
Timely filing The claim was submitted too late under plan rules. Ask for the original submission date, proof of filing, and whether the provider — not the patient — caused the delay.
Duplicate claim / already processed The insurer thinks the same claim was submitted twice or already paid. Compare claim numbers, dates of service, and EOBs to make sure a corrected claim was not mistaken for a duplicate.
Coordination of benefits / other insurance issue The insurer thinks another plan should pay first, or your insurance information does not match. Call both plans, update other-insurance records, and ask the provider to resubmit after COB is corrected.
Coding error / invalid code / bundling The claim may have been billed with the wrong CPT, ICD-10, modifier, or claim format. Ask the provider’s billing office for a coding review and whether a corrected claim can be submitted before you appeal.

Got a Surprise Medical Bill?
Here's What You Need to Know.

A federal law called the No Surprises Act went into effect in January 2022. It was designed to protect you from getting blindsided by huge medical bills you never expected — and never agreed to. Here's everything you need to know, in plain English.

⚡ Quick Cheat Sheet — Your Top Rights at a Glance

  • You cannot be billed more than your in-network rate for most emergency services — even if the doctor or hospital is out-of-network.
  • You cannot be balance billed for out-of-network doctors at an in-network hospital during a non-emergency procedure.
  • You cannot be charged surprise fees for air ambulance rides from out-of-network providers.
  • If you have no insurance, providers must give you a cost estimate before they treat you.
  • If your actual bill is $400+ more than your estimate, you have the right to dispute it.
  • You can call 1-800-985-3059 right now if you've received a surprise bill.
Section 1

Wait — What Even Is a Surprise Bill?

Imagine you go to the emergency room. You check that the hospital takes your insurance. You think you're covered. Then a few weeks later, a bill shows up — not from the hospital, but from a doctor you never even chose, like the radiologist who read your X-ray or the anesthesiologist who put you to sleep during surgery. That doctor is out-of-network. And now they want you to pay the difference between what your insurance paid and what they billed. That difference? That's the surprise bill.

It's also called balance billing — because they're billing you for the leftover balance your insurance didn't cover. It can be hundreds or even thousands of dollars. And in most cases, it's now illegal.

Section 2

What Does the No Surprises Act Actually Cover?

This law protects you in three main situations:

🚨 Emergency Room Visits

If you go to the ER because you genuinely believe something is seriously wrong (chest pain, a bad injury, trouble breathing), you are protected — no matter which doctors treat you, even if they're out-of-network. Your out-of-pocket cost cannot be more than what you'd pay for an in-network doctor.

🏥 Out-of-Network Doctors at an In-Network Hospital

If you're having a planned procedure at a hospital that takes your insurance, but some of the doctors involved (like the anesthesiologist or assistant surgeon) are out-of-network, you cannot be surprise billed for them. You only pay your normal in-network rates.

🚁 Air Ambulance Rides

If you had to be airlifted to a hospital, surprise bills for out-of-network air ambulance services are banned. You only pay what you'd owe for an in-network service — even if there are no in-network air ambulances in your area.

Section 3

Does This Law Apply to Me?

The No Surprises Act covers most people with health insurance in the U.S. Here's a quick breakdown:

✓ You ARE Protected If You Have...

  • Insurance through your job
  • A plan you bought on healthcare.gov or your state marketplace
  • A plan you bought directly from an insurance company
  • Coverage through a school (student health plan)
  • Federal employee health benefits (FEHB)
  • A plan through a state or local government employer

✗ You Are NOT Covered If You Have...

  • Medicare or Medicaid (they have their own protections)
  • Veterans Affairs (VA) health care
  • TRICARE (military coverage)
  • Short-term health insurance
  • A retiree-only plan
  • Stand-alone dental or vision plans

Note: If Medicare or Medicaid is your coverage, you still have protections — they're just handled under different rules. You should still call the help desk number at the bottom of this page.

Section 4

A Real-Life Example: What This Looks Like

📖 Meet Jordan — Age 19

The ER Visit That Turned Into a $2,400 Surprise

Jordan is 19 and a first-year college student. One night, he wakes up with severe chest pain. He's scared it might be a heart attack. He grabs an Uber to the nearest ER — the same hospital that's listed as in-network on his parents' insurance plan.

The ER doctors run tests, do an EKG, and call in a cardiologist to read his results. Good news: it's a severe panic attack, not a heart attack. Jordan is relieved and goes home that night.

Six weeks later, two bills show up in the mail. One is from the hospital — expected, no problem. The second is from the cardiologist who read his test results. That doctor is out-of-network. The bill says Jordan owes $2,400. Nobody told Jordan this doctor was out-of-network. He never chose that doctor. He had no idea.

✓ Under the No Surprises Act — Jordan doesn't owe that $2,400. Because Jordan went to the ER with symptoms he genuinely believed were serious, and the cardiologist was part of his emergency care at an in-network hospital, that doctor cannot bill Jordan more than his normal in-network rate. Jordan called the No Surprises Help Desk at 1-800-985-3059, filed a complaint, and the bill was corrected. He ended up paying only his normal copay.
Section 5

The Catches — What the Law Doesn't Cover (And How Insurers Exploit It)

The No Surprises Act is a real step forward. But it has gaps — and big insurance companies know exactly where those gaps are.

Ground Ambulances Are NOT Covered

If you're transported by a regular ambulance (not an air ambulance), the No Surprises Act does not protect you. Ground ambulance surprise billing is still completely legal. This is one of the biggest gaps in the law and disproportionately harms people in rural areas.

Out-of-Network Facilities Are NOT Covered

If the entire hospital or facility you go to is out-of-network for a non-emergency visit, the law generally doesn't protect you. The law kicks in mainly when you're at an in-network facility and the individual doctor is out-of-network.

You Can Be Tricked Into Signing Away Your Rights

In some non-emergency situations, a provider can ask you to sign a "notice and consent" form — which lets them opt out of the No Surprises Act protections and balance bill you anyway. Know your rights before you sign anything. More on this below.

⚠️ POPNYC Watchdog Alert — UnitedHealth Group

How Big Insurers Use This Law Against You

The No Surprises Act created an arbitration process — a way for doctors and insurance companies to settle billing disputes behind closed doors. UnitedHealth Group (UHG), the largest health insurer in the U.S., has been accused of aggressively using this arbitration system to lowball payments to doctors and hospitals.

Here's why that matters to you: When insurers underpay doctors through arbitration, those doctors have less incentive to stay in-network. Fewer in-network doctors = more situations where you're forced to see out-of-network providers — creating the exact surprise bill problem the law was meant to fix.

  • UHG has one of the highest claim denial rates of any major insurer in the U.S.
  • Their AI tool (nH Predict) has been documented denying medically necessary care at scale
  • Their provider networks have been found to list incorrect or outdated in-network doctors — meaning patients go to who they think is in-network, only to get a surprise bill
  • Under the No Surprises Act, if a provider directory has wrong information and you get an out-of-network bill as a result, you are entitled to a full refund
Section 6

Don't Sign Away Your Rights — What Is "Notice and Consent"?

In certain non-emergency situations, a provider can ask you to sign a form that lets them charge you out-of-network rates — even when the No Surprises Act would normally protect you. This is called a Notice and Consent exception.

Before you sign anything like this, know that:

They CANNOT ask you to sign this during an emergency

If you are being evaluated, stabilized, or treated in an emergency, any notice and consent form you sign is not valid. You cannot legally waive your rights when you're in crisis.

They CANNOT ask you to sign for ancillary services

Ancillary services are things like anesthesiology, radiology, lab work, and pathology — services you typically don't get to choose yourself. You cannot be asked to waive your rights for these.

They MUST give you a cost estimate first

Before you sign any consent form to waive your protections, the provider is legally required to give you a written Good Faith Estimate of what they plan to charge you.

Section 7

What If I Don't Have Insurance?

The No Surprises Act also has protections for people who are uninsured or paying out of pocket (self-pay). Here's what you're entitled to:

  • You have the right to a Good Faith Estimate — a written cost estimate before you receive care. Ask for it. Providers are legally required to give it to you.
  • The estimate must be provided within 1 business day if your appointment is 3–9 days out, or within 3 business days if it's 10+ days out.
  • If your final bill comes in at $400 or more above the estimate, you have the right to dispute it through the Patient-Provider Dispute Resolution process.
  • Any provider or facility — hospital, clinic, urgent care, imaging center, lab — is required to comply with these rules.
Section 8

Your Full List of Rights Under the No Surprises Act

  • You cannot be charged more than your in-network cost share for most emergency services
  • You cannot be balance billed by out-of-network providers at in-network facilities
  • You cannot be required to get prior authorization before receiving emergency care
  • Your insurer must cover emergency services even at out-of-network facilities
  • You have the right to a Good Faith Estimate before planned care (insured or not)
  • You have the right to dispute any bill that is $400+ above your estimate
  • You have the right to an external review if your insurance claim is denied
  • You have the right to accurate provider directory information — if you were misled by a wrong directory and got an out-of-network bill, you are entitled to a refund
  • If your doctor leaves your insurance network mid-treatment for a serious condition, you have the right to up to 90 days of continued care at in-network rates
  • Your insurance ID card must clearly show your deductible, out-of-pocket maximum, and contact information
Section 9

Got a Surprise Bill Right Now? Here's What to Do.

Don't panic. Don't pay it yet. Follow these steps:

Don't ignore it — but don't pay it yet

Paying a bill can sometimes be treated as accepting it. Read the bill carefully first. Write down the date you received it and every provider and service listed.

Check if the No Surprises Act covers your situation

Was it an emergency? Were you at an in-network hospital? Was the surprising charge from a doctor you didn't choose? If yes to any of these, you're likely protected.

Call the No Surprises Help Desk

Call 1-800-985-3059 or file a complaint online at cms.gov/medical-bill-rights. They can walk you through your options and help you file a formal complaint.

Contact your insurance company

Call the member services number on your insurance card and explain the situation. Ask them to confirm whether the No Surprises Act applies to your bill. Write down the name of who you spoke to and the date.

File an internal appeal with your insurer (if denied)

If your insurer disagrees and you think they're wrong, you have the right to file a formal internal appeal. Ask your insurer for the appeals process in writing.

Request an External Review if your appeal is denied

If your internal appeal is denied, you can take it to an independent third party reviewer. Go to externalappeal.cms.gov or call 888-866-6205.

Contact your state's Consumer Assistance Program (if available)

Some states have free programs that help patients fight surprise bills and navigate insurance appeals. Check if your state has one at cms.gov.

How to tell: ask HR “Is our plan self-funded or fully-insured?”, check your SPD, or look for “administered by” wording.
Why it matters for my complaint
If you complain to the wrong regulator you’ll lose weeks. Self-funded → the U.S. Department of Labor (EBSA). Fully-insured → your state insurance department. When in doubt, contact the EBSA Benefits Advisor at 1-866-444-3272 — they’ll tell you which lane you’re in.
Get your plan documents (SPD)
You have a right to your Summary Plan Description and full plan document. They spell out covered benefits, exclusions, and appeal deadlines. Use the SPD request template — the plan administrator generally must provide it.

How to use this document: Work through the steps in order. Step 3 tells you exactly which evidence to attach for your denial type. Step 4 gives you ready-to-adapt letters. Step 5 is your deadline master table — check it the day you receive a denial. Steps 7–8 map the full escalation ladder, including when and how to involve the federal government. Step 1 — In the First 48 Hours — Triage the Denial

  • Pull the Explanation of Benefits (EOB) or denial/adverse-determination notice — do not rely on a phone-call summary.
  • Identify which UnitedHealthcare product line you’re in (process, deadlines, and contacts differ): Medicare Advantage (Part C) / Part D · Employer / commercial (fully insured or self-funded/ERISA) · ACA Marketplace · UHC Community Plan (Medicaid).
  • Identify the denial reason and remark code on the EOB (e.g., CO-96 medical necessity, CO-197 prior auth, CO-29 timely filing, CO-97/CO-4/CO-16/CO-22 coding) — this sets which evidence packet (Step 3) you need.
  • Write the date printed on the notice on your calendar — appeal clocks run from that date, not when you opened the mail.
  • Note whether this is pre-service (care not yet received) or post-service — you cannot expedite a post-service claim.
  • If your health could be seriously jeopardized by waiting, flag it for an expedited/urgent appeal immediately (Step 4 script).

Step 2 — Build Your Medical Necessity Evidence File

Assemble this before writing any appeal letter — nearly every denial type draws from it.
Identification & administrative
  • Copy of the denial notice / EOB (every page)
  • Member ID, group number, plan type (PPO/HMO/POS/Medicare Advantage/Community Plan)
  • Claim number, CPT/HCPCS codes, ICD-10 diagnosis codes, date(s) of service
  • Prior authorization number and approval documentation, if one was issued
  • Signed authorization if a spouse/advocate/attorney is submitting for the member (CMS Form 1696 for Medicare; UHC’s authorized-representative form for commercial/MA)
Clinical documentation
  • Physician’s letter of medical necessity — the single most influential document
  • Office visit notes documenting diagnosis, functional limitations, and objective findings
  • Diagnostic results: labs, imaging, pathology
  • Records of prior treatments tried, duration, and why they failed (needed for step-therapy disputes)
  • Specialist consultation notes and discharge summaries, if applicable
Coverage-policy evidence
  • The specific UHC Coverage Determination Guideline (CDG) cited in the denial (UHCprovider.com → Clinical Resources → CDGs); quote the exact criteria and show line-by-line how the record satisfies each
  • Relevant medical-society clinical guideline excerpts (with year/version)
  • 1–2 supporting peer-reviewed studies, if directly on point
  • The plan’s Evidence of Coverage / Certificate of Coverage / SPD section that supports coverage
Proof-of-submission trail (start now)
  • Certified-mail receipt or fax confirmation for every submission
  • Portal confirmation numbers from UHCprovider.com or myuhc.com
  • A running log: date sent, method, confirmation number, who you spoke with, what was said (Step 9 template)

Step 7 — Escalation Path

Medicare Advantage (5 statutory levels)
  • 1 — Reconsideration by the plan (UHC) — 60 days to file
  • 2 — Independent Review Entity (C2C Innovative Solutions, as of May 1 2026) — automatic if the plan upholds its denial
  • 3 — Administrative Law Judge (OMHA) — 60 days to request; $200 amount-in-controversy minimum (2026)
  • 4 — Medicare Appeals Council — 60 days; no dollar minimum
  • 5 — U.S. District Court — 60 days; $1,960 minimum (2026)
Employer-Funded / ERISA Plans
  • Internal appeal to UHC (at least 180 days to file)
  • Second-level internal appeal, if your plan document offers one (check your SPD)
  • External review by an IRO — required for non-grandfathered plans; file within 4 months of the final internal denial; decision in 45 days (standard) / 72 hours (expedited); binding if it overturns the denial
  • Department of Labor / EBSA — see Step 8
  • Lawsuit under ERISA §502(a)(1)(B) — only after internal remedies are exhausted (or “deemed exhausted”)

Step 8 — When & How to Escalate to the Department of Labor (ERISA plans only)

EBSA does not re-adjudicate your medical denial — it enforces whether your plan followed the ERISA claims-procedure rules (29 CFR §2560.503-1): timely decisions, adequate notices, your right to documents.
Contact EBSA when:
  • UHC misses a required decision deadline (e.g., doesn’t decide within 30/60 days)
  • Your denial notice fails to explain the specific reason, the CDG/criteria used, or your appeal rights
  • You’re denied access to your claim file or plan documents (SPD, claims procedures)
  • You suspect the plan’s process itself violates federal claims regulations
How to contact EBSA: Phone 1-866-444-3272 (Benefits Advisors) · Online askebsa.dol.gov (status updates ~every 30 days) · Mail: U.S. DOL, EBSA, Division of Technical Assistance & Inquiries, 200 Constitution Ave NW, Suite N-5619, Washington, DC 20210.

Your right to sue (ERISA §502(a)(1)(B)): You generally must exhaust internal appeal (and external review) first — unless the plan failed to follow proper procedures (“deemed exhausted”). Since 2018, plans must disclose the lawsuit filing deadline in the denial letter itself; if yours doesn’t, that omission may be worth raising with EBSA. Consult an ERISA attorney before filing — this is where representation meaningfully changes outcomes.

Step 5 — Cross-Payer Escalation Path

Medicare Advantage — 5 statutory levels (identical across all payers; set by CMS)
  • Plan reconsideration — file within 60–65 days per the payer block
  • Independent Review Entity (IRE) — automatic if the plan upholds. As of May 1 2026 the Part C IRE is C2C Innovative Solutions: PO Box 1949, Jacksonville, FL 32231-0053; Fax (904) 539-4080; 1-866-320-0963
  • ALJ hearing (OMHA) — 60 days; 2026 threshold $200
  • Medicare Appeals Council — 60 days; no dollar threshold
  • Federal District Court — 60 days; 2026 threshold $1,960
Commercial / Employer (ERISA) — universal path
  • Internal appeal to the insurer (180 days is the ERISA/ACA federal floor; levels vary)
  • Second-level internal appeal, if your SPD offers one
  • External review by an IRO — file within 4 months of the final internal denial; 45 days standard / 72 hours expedited
  • Note: as of July 1 2026 the HHS-Administered Federal External Review Process (FERP) is temporarily unavailable in states that rely on it (e.g., AL, FL, GA, TX, WI) — call the number on your denial letter to confirm the path
  • Department of Labor / EBSA — see Step 6
  • Lawsuit under ERISA §502(a)(1)(B) — after internal remedies are exhausted
California Kaiser members — an additional parallel path: request DMHC Independent Medical Review (IMR) — often faster and free, available *without* finishing Kaiser's internal grievance if it's an emergency, unresolved after 30 days, experimental/investigational, or an imminent serious threat.

  • Your denial notice fails to explain the specific reason, the clinical policy used, or your appeal rights
  • You're denied access to your claim file or plan documents

⚠️ TRAP #1 — INPATIENT VS. OBSERVATION STATUS  ·  The #1 Most Financially Dangerous Gray Area in Hospital Billing
What You Need to Know Link Why It Matters Action
Observation Status vs. Inpatient Admission
BILLING TRAP
 Medicare.gov →
Hospitals can keep you overnight in a real bed with nurses and tests — and still classify you as "outpatient under observation." To you it feels identical. The bill is completely different. Observation = billed as outpatient, meaning you may owe 20% of every single service, every drug, every test. For Medicare patients: observation status does NOT count toward the 3-day inpatient requirement for Medicare-covered skilled nursing facility care afterward. This can result in surprise bills of thousands of dollars for what felt like a routine overnight stay.
Ask the moment you check in:
"Am I formally admitted as inpatient, or am I under observation status?"
Ask for it IN WRITING.
MOON Form — Medicare Outpatient Observation Notice
MEDICARE RIGHT
 CMS MOON Info →
Under the NOTICE Act (effective 2016), hospitals MUST give Medicare patients written notice within 36 hours if they are under observation status. This form is called the Medicare Outpatient Observation Notice (MOON). Private insurance patients: there is currently NO federal law requiring hospitals to notify you. You must ask. All patients: you have the right to ask at any point during your stay what your official admission status is.
 CMS.gov →
The Two-Midnight Rule — and the Timing Trap
CMS RULE
 CMS Rule →
A federal CMS rule: if a doctor reasonably expects you to need hospital care spanning at least 2 midnights, you should be formally admitted as inpatient. Less than 2 midnights = outpatient billing. The trap: hospitals bill by calendar days, not 24-hour periods. Arriving Monday at 8pm and leaving Tuesday afternoon = 2 calendar days = potentially billed as inpatient OR as 2 outpatient days. Arriving Tuesday at 3am and leaving Tuesday afternoon = 1 day = 1 outpatient day. A difference of just a few hours in arrival time can mean a dramatically different bill.
Ask your doctor: "Is there any billing reason I should arrive closer to the procedure time rather than the evening before?"
📋 QUESTIONS TO ASK BEFORE ANY HOSPITAL STAY OR PROCEDURE
ABOUT YOUR PROCEDURE
  • "Will this be billed as inpatient or outpatient?"
  • "Is there a freestanding outpatient surgery center where this can be done?" (Hospital outpatient rates are typically 40–60% higher)
  • "What is the CPT code for my procedure?" (Use it to look up your coverage before you go)
  • "Will I need to stay overnight? If so, inpatient or observation status?"
ABOUT YOUR INSURANCE
  • "Has my insurance pre-authorized this procedure?" (If the hospital hasn't gotten it, you may be on the hook)
  • "Is this hospital AND are these doctors in-network for my specific plan?" (A hospital can be in-network while its anesthesiologist is out-of-network)
  • "What is my out-of-pocket maximum, and how much have I already met this year?"
ABOUT THE BILL
  • "Can I get an itemized estimate of charges before my procedure?"
  • "Does this hospital have a financial assistance or charity care program? Can I apply regardless of income?"
  • "What is your cash-pay or self-pay discount rate?" (Even insured patients can sometimes negotiate this)
  • "Is there a facility fee on top of the procedure fee?"
ABOUT TIMING
  • "Is there any billing advantage to arriving at a specific time?"
  • "If I arrive the evening before a morning procedure, how will that overnight stay be billed?"
  • "Will my admission status change based on when I arrive?"
⚖️ NO SURPRISES ACT — WHAT IT COVERS (AND WHAT IT DOESN'T)
✅ WHAT IT PROTECTS YOU FROM (effective Jan 1, 2022)
  • Surprise bills from out-of-network ER doctors
  • Surprise bills from out-of-network providers at in-network facilities (like the anesthesiologist you never chose)
  • Air ambulance surprise bills from out-of-network providers
  • Good Faith Estimate required BEFORE any scheduled procedure — ask for it at least 3 business days in advance
  • If final bill is more than $400 above your Good Faith Estimate, you can dispute it through federal IDR
❌ WHAT IT DOES NOT COVER — CURRENT GAPS
  • Ground ambulance — still a major loophole. Federal protections as of 2026 are still limited. You CAN be balance-billed.
  • Scheduled procedures where you knowingly chose out-of-network care
  • Your deductible, copay, and coinsurance — those still apply
  • Balance billing from providers you explicitly consented to use out-of-network
  • Most birthing centers, clinics, hospice, addiction treatment, nursing homes, urgent care (OON non-emergency)
File NSA complaints at: cms.gov/nosurprises  ·  IDR disputes: nsa-idr.cms.gov/consumercomplaints
👤 SPECIAL SITUATIONS — WHAT YOU'RE ENTITLED TO BY PLAN TYPE
IF YOU HAVE MEDICARE
  • Always ask for the MOON form if staying overnight — you are legally entitled to it
  • Observation status does NOT count toward your 3-day inpatient requirement for skilled nursing coverage
  • You can appeal an observation status classification — ask your doctor to request inpatient admission and document medical necessity
  • Helpline: 1-800-MEDICARE
IF YOU HAVE MEDICAID
  • You generally CANNOT be balance-billed by Medicaid-participating providers
  • Inpatient and outpatient are covered differently — contact your state Medicaid office for specifics
  • If you're denied a service, you have the right to a fair hearing — request one immediately
IF YOU HAVE PRIVATE/EMPLOYER INSURANCE
  • Your plan's Summary of Benefits and Coverage (SBC) must tell you exactly what you owe for inpatient vs. outpatient — get this before your procedure
  • You have the right to internal appeal of any denial, followed by external review
  • No Surprises Act protects you from most out-of-network surprise bills
IF YOU ARE UNINSURED
  • All nonprofit hospitals (which is most U.S. hospitals) are legally required to have charity care programs
  • Ask for the hospital's Financial Assistance Policy (FAP) before or during your stay
  • Hospitals often reduce bills by 30–60% for uninsured patients who ask for the self-pay discount rate
  • Dollar For (dollarfor.org) can help you apply — free, takes ~20 minutes

Prior authorization required / no authorizationThe plan says approval was needed before the service, scan, surgery, or medication.Ask the provider whether they requested auth, get the denial notice, and ask for a retro authorization or medical-necessity review if appropriate.

📝 How to Appeal a Denial Source: HealthCare.gov · CMS External Review INTERNAL + EXTERNAL REVIEW · STEP-BY-STEP
⚖️ Your Right to Appeal
If your insurer refuses to pay a claim or ends your coverage, you have the right to appeal and have the decision reviewed. Insurers must tell you why they denied your claim and how to dispute it. There are two paths — and if your case is urgent, the process must be sped up:
  • Internal appeal: ask your insurance company to do a full and fair review of its own decision.
  • External review: take it to an independent third party. The insurer no longer gets the final say and is required by law to accept the result.
KNOW YOUR RIGHTSFREE OR LOW-COST
🗂️ Step-by-Step: Request Your Claim File
ProPublica Helper →
Your claim file contains every document tied to your claim — internal notes, medical reviews, and the insurer's own decision-making records (which they're legally required to keep). It's the single most powerful tool for an appeal: it reveals the insurer's own evidence and any errors or procedure violations.
  • Use a template: generate a formal request letter with ProPublica's Claim File Helper.
  • Submit it: send to your insurer's appeals or medical-records department by certified mail.
  • Know the deadline: under HIPAA's Privacy Rule, insurers must provide it within 30 days.
  • If they don't comply: escalate to your state Department of Insurance (DOI) with a formal complaint — JusticeDirect has step-by-step guides.
  • Document everything: log every call, email, and letter with dates and names.

1️⃣ Internal Appeal — The 3 Steps
Insurer portals →
  • File a claim (a request for coverage). You or your provider submits it.
  • The plan denies it in writing. The insurer must notify you within 15 days (prior authorization), 30 days (services already received), or 72 hours (urgent care).
  • You file the internal appeal — complete the insurer's forms (or write with your name, claim number, and ID), and add supporting info like a doctor's letter.
You have 180 days (6 months) from the denial to file. Decisions: within 30 days (service not yet received) or 60 days (already received). Your state's CAP can file for you.
Start here: insurer appeal portals · appeal packets · letter templates · HealthCare.gov guide

2️⃣ External Review — Independent & Binding
File a review →
An independent third party reviews your case, and your insurer is legally required to accept the decision.
  • File within 4 months of the final denial / internal-appeal determination.
  • Standard reviews: decided within 45 days. Expedited (urgent): within 72 hours or less.
  • Cost: free through the HHS-administered process; if a state process or independent review organization is used, no more than $25.
  • Urgent cases: you can request external review at the same time as your internal appeal.
File it: externalappeal.cms.gov · CMS external review · state process via your state DOI

📖 Key Laws & Your Rights Sources: CMS · DOL EBSA · HHS 6 LAWS · WITH ENFORCEMENT CONTACTS
The No Surprises Act (2022)
cms.gov/nosurprises →
42 U.S.C. § 300gg-111. Prohibits surprise billing for emergency and ancillary services from out-of-network providers at in-network facilities. Enforced by CMS, HHS, and state insurance commissioners. Report violations by calling the help desk or filing with your state DOI.

The NOTICE Act — Observation Status
MOON form →
42 U.S.C. § 1320d-8. Requires Medicare hospitals to inform patients in writing (the MOON form) within 36 hours when they are under "observation" status rather than admitted — a distinction that can cost thousands. Enforced by CMS and Medicare.

Report: 1-800-633-4227 (1-800-MEDICARE)
Affordable Care Act (ACA)
healthcare.gov →
42 U.S.C. § 18001+. Bans denial for pre-existing conditions, requires coverage of essential health benefits, guarantees the right to appeal denials, provides subsidies for low-income enrollees, and mandates mental health parity. Enforced by CMS, HHS, and state insurance commissioners.

Marketplace: 1-800-318-2596
Mental Health Parity Act
Learn more →
42 U.S.C. § 300gg-26. Requires insurance to cover mental health and substance-use treatment no more restrictively than medical and surgical care. Enforced by CMS and state insurance commissioners. Report violations to your state DOI or the No Surprises help line.

Report: 1-800-985-3059
ERISA (1974)
dol.gov/ebsa →
29 U.S.C. § 1001+. Governs employer-sponsored health plans and protects your right to a full claims-appeal process. The most powerful option for denials under a job-based plan. Enforced by the U.S. Department of Labor's EBSA.

HIPAA
hhs.gov/hipaa →
45 CFR § 164. Protects the privacy of your medical information and guarantees your right to access your own medical records — providers must respond within 30 days. Enforced by the HHS Office for Civil Rights.

🏢 Contact the Insurers & Escalate to the Top Verified X accounts · official press contacts · executive-escalation routes 6 INSURERS
📧 Going to the top works. After losing standard appeals of a denial for her cancer treatment, writer Elizabeth Nicholas emailed her insurer's CEO and communications director — the treatment was approved within about a day (Vanity Fair; see also MedPage Today, Wendell Potter). A direct, documented email to an executive is a legitimate, well-known escalation lever — use it for your own care.
How to write it (the approach that works):
  • Email the executive / office-of-the-president address AND the communications/press contact together — that combination is what moved Nicholas's case.
  • Identify yourself as the patient: full name, member ID, and claim number.
  • Attach the denial letter and your doctor's letter of medical necessity.
  • State the exact action you want ("approve [treatment] denied on [date]") and a reasonable deadline.
  • If true, note that you are documenting the denial and prepared to share your story with the press or your state regulator.
  • Keep it short and factual. Start with the executive-response address; if there's no reply in a few business days, move up to the CEO — don't blast everyone at once.

Who do you call? It depends on where your insurance came from.

🏛️ Got insurance yourself, through Healthcare.gov, or from a small employer? → Call your state insurance department listed below. It's free, and they can investigate your insurer.

🏢 Got insurance through a large employer? → Your plan is probably "self-funded," meaning the company pays claims itself and uses an insurer just for paperwork. A federal law called ERISA covers these plans — and your state has absolutely no authority over them. Calling your state department will get you nowhere. Instead, call the U.S. Department of Labor (EBSA) at 1-866-444-3272.

💡 How do you know which type you have? Call your HR department and ask: "Is our health plan self-funded or fully-insured?" If self-funded → DOL EBSA. If fully-insured → state department below.

📋 When filing any state complaint, bring: your denial letter · your insurance policy · a letter from your doctor explaining why the care was medically necessary. Timeline: 4–12 weeks. Cost: FREE.

⚠️ What Is a Facility Fee?
A facility fee is a hidden charge hospitals add on top of your doctor's bill just because the office you visited is owned by a hospital. You can go to the exact same doctor, get the exact same care — and pay hundreds or thousands more simply because the hospital bought that doctor's practice. These fees are often not covered by insurance and come as a surprise bill weeks later.

🚑 Ground Ambulance Billing Sources: CMS GAPB · Commonwealth Fund · legalclarity.org THE BIGGEST REMAINING SURPRISE-BILL LOOPHOLE
🚨 Current Federal Status: NO PROTECTION
Ground ambulances are the single biggest remaining loophole in federal surprise-billing protection. Zero federal law, patchwork state coverage — most Americans are completely exposed.
  • The No Surprises Act (2022) deliberately excluded ground ambulances.
  • The federal GAPB Advisory Committee issued its final report on August 28, 2024, recommending Congress prohibit balance billing for emergency ground ambulance — but the committee is now inactive and no federal legislation has passed.
  • Bills range from $1,200 to over $3,000. An estimated 71% of ambulance rides risk a surprise bill.
NO FEDERAL LAW71% OF RIDES AT RISK
🏔️ Colorado — A Case Study in Political Failure
  • Over 2/3 of emergency ground ambulance rides in Colorado are out-of-network — among the worst rates in the country.
  • Existing partial protection covers only private ambulance services — but most are public (fire departments, county services).
  • In 2025 the legislature passed a fix unanimously in both chambers — and the Governor vetoed it over insurance-premium concerns.
  • As of May 2026, Colorado residents have no meaningful protection.

✅ What You Can Do Right Now
  • Before non-emergency transport: ask if the provider is in-network — non-emergency transport can sometimes be scheduled and negotiated.
  • After a bill: call the ambulance company and ask for their financial assistance policy — many have one and don't advertise it.
  • Negotiate: companies routinely reduce bills for patients who ask directly, especially the uninsured.
  • File a complaint with your state insurance commissioner — even without legal protection it creates a public record and adds pressure for reform.
